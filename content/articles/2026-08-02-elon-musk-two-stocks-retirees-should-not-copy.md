Asked what he'd invest in, Elon Musk gave the answer only a man worth several hundred billion dollars can afford to give. He said he doesn't really buy stocks, he builds companies. Then he said Google "is gonna be pretty valuable" and that Nvidia "is obvious at this point," because AI and robotics will end up producing "almost all the value" in the economy.

Financial newsletters ran it as a stock tip. We'll run it as a warning, because the gap between Musk's situation and yours is the whole story.

## What Musk can do that you can't

Musk's wealth is in companies he controls. If Tesla drops 50%, he doesn't sell a share; he borrows against them. He is 55. He has no mortgage payment that depends on a portfolio, no Medicare premium coming out of a fixed check, and no need to convert stock into groceries this year or any year. A bet that goes to zero doesn't change his life.

A retiree who puts a meaningful slice of savings into two stocks because the world's richest man likes them is taking the same risk with none of the same padding. If the bet works, you get a little richer. If it doesn't, you get poorer at the exact age when there's no earning your way back.

> **What to do:** Any single stock should be small enough that losing all of it wouldn't change a single thing about your year. For most retirees that's under 5% of savings per company, and that's for money you've already decided you can lose.

## You already own both

Here's what the tip-sheets don't mention. Google's parent, Alphabet, and Nvidia are two of the largest companies in the S&P 500. Between them they make up something like a tenth of the index. If you own a total-market or S&P 500 fund, you already have a big position in both, and it grows automatically as they grow.

That's Warren Buffett's whole argument for index funds in one example. You get Musk's picks without Musk's concentration, and if he's wrong about robotics, the other 498 companies are still there.

## The AI bet, honestly

Musk might be right that AI and robotics reshape the economy. Smart people thought the same about railroads in 1870, radio in 1928, and the internet in 1999. All three were right about the technology and wrong about which companies would capture the money, and investors who bought the obvious winners at the top lost most of it. Nvidia has roughly tripled in three years and trades at a valuation that assumes the boom continues. Maybe it will. "Obvious" is what things look like right before they aren't.

> **What to do:** If you want more AI exposure than the index gives you, add a small, capped position and write down today's date and the price. When you check it in a year, you'll know whether you were investing or gambling.

## Who this tip is actually for

The interview clip circulated alongside ads for gold IRAs, "pre-IPO" shares, and fractional art, all pitched as ways to "invest like Elon." Musk's own advice in the same interview was that he doesn't invest at all. He builds things. He can't be copied, and the people selling you a way to copy him know it.

A retiree's job isn't to find the next Nvidia. It's to make sure the money lasts as long as you do. Musk never has to worry about that. You do. Own the whole market, keep two years of bills in Treasury bills, and let the richest man alive take the risks he can afford.
