Elon Musk's fortune didn't come from a brokerage account. "I don't really buy stocks," he told an interviewer. "I just try to build things." So when he was pressed on which companies he'd buy purely to make money, the answer got attention.

Google, he said, "is gonna be pretty valuable in the future," because it has "laid the groundwork for an immense amount of value creation" in artificial intelligence. And Nvidia, the company whose chips power nearly every AI system on earth, "is obvious at this point."

His reasoning is bigger than two stocks. Musk believes companies that do AI and robotics will end up producing "almost all the value" in the economy, and that the output from them "will dwarf everything else." If he's even half right, the two names he picked sit at the center of it.

## Why the thesis deserves a hearing

Musk runs the largest AI compute buildout of any private company, sells robots and self-driving software, and buys Nvidia chips by the hundred thousand. When he says Nvidia is obvious, he's describing his own purchase orders. When he says Google is positioned, he's describing a competitor he's spent years trying to beat. That's not a stock tip from a TV pundit. It's a read from inside the industry.

And the market has largely agreed. Nvidia has roughly tripled in three years and Alphabet is one of the five largest companies in the world. The question for a retiree isn't whether Musk is smart. It's how much of your money should ride on him being right.

## The part of his situation you don't share

Musk is 55. His wealth is in companies he controls, and when they fall he borrows against them rather than selling. He has no Medicare premium coming out of a fixed check and no year in which he must turn stock into groceries. A bet that goes to zero doesn't change his life.

A retiree who moves a large share of savings into two stocks is taking his risk without his cushion. Smart people were right about railroads in 1870, radio in 1928, and the internet in 1999, and wrong about which companies would keep the money. Investors who bought the obvious winners at the top lost most of it. "Obvious" is what things look like right before they aren't, and a retiree can't earn a bad decade back.

> **What to do:** Any single company should be small enough that losing all of it wouldn't change a thing about your year. For most retirees that's under 5% of savings per stock, and only with money you've already decided you can afford to lose.

## The good news: you probably already own them

Alphabet and Nvidia are two of the largest holdings in the S&P 500. Between them they're something like a tenth of the index. If you own a total-market or S&P 500 fund, you already have a meaningful position in both of Musk's picks, and it grows automatically as they grow, with the other 498 companies as a cushion if AI takes longer than he thinks.

That's the whole case for an index fund in one example: you get the winners without having to guess them.

## If you want more than the index gives you

- **Add a capped position.** A few percent in each, written down with the date and price, so in a year you can tell whether you invested or gambled.
- **Use a diversified AI or technology fund** rather than two tickers, so a single earnings miss can't take the whole position down.
- **Pair it with what Musk isn't telling you to buy.** Two years of spending in Treasury bills, so a bad stretch for tech never forces you to sell at the bottom.
- **Talk to a fee-only fiduciary** before moving more than a small slice. Musk's timeline and risk tolerance are not yours, and a one-hour review costs less than one bad quarter.

> **What to do:** Look up what percentage of your largest fund is already in Alphabet and Nvidia. Most retirees are surprised. Then decide how much more, if any, you want, and write the number down before the next headline moves it for you.

Musk may well be right about where the value goes. Owning the whole market, with a little extra where you have conviction and enough cash to never be forced out, is how a retiree gets to be right with him without needing to be.
