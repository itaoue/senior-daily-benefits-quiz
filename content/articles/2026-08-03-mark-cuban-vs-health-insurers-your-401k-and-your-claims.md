Payton Herres had a heart transplant as a child. For years her insurer covered the generic anti-rejection drug she takes every day. Then it stopped. She posted on Facebook. The post went viral. The insurer restored coverage, at $1,000 per 90-day supply instead of the $180 she'd been paying. "Ghost approval," she called it. Approved on paper, unaffordable in practice.

Mark Cuban saw the story and did two things. His pharmacy, Cost Plus Drugs, now supplies her drug for about $300. And he went on a tear, telling Americans that if their 401(k) holds a broad index fund, they own UnitedHealth, Cigna, CVS, and Elevance, and are therefore "part of the cost of health care problem."

On the first thing, he's a hero. On the second, we think he's aiming at the wrong target, and it matters because his advice, followed literally, would hurt the retirees he's trying to help.

## Where Cuban is right

American health insurers make money by collecting premiums and not paying claims. That's the model. Denials, prior authorizations, and "ghost approvals" at prices no one can pay aren't a glitch; they're the product. Herres got her medication back because strangers made enough noise to reach a billionaire. Most people don't get a Mark Cuban.

And yes, the largest insurers are in every broad index fund. Vanguard's total market fund holds all six of the big ones. So does the S&P 500. If you own the market, you own them.

## Where he's wrong

Selling your index fund doesn't change a single denial. UnitedHealth doesn't notice when a retiree in Ohio moves $80,000 from one fund to another. The stock has other buyers. Meanwhile, the retiree has given up the cheapest, most diversified investment available to switch into something narrower, more expensive, and more likely to be sold by a person with a commission. The "ethical" funds marketed to fill that gap charge five to ten times the fees for a portfolio that still holds plenty of companies you wouldn't like if you looked.

Owning the market isn't an endorsement of everything in it. It's a refusal to pretend you can pick. Cuban knows that. He built a pharmacy to fight insurers, not a mutual fund.

> **What to do:** Keep the index fund. If you want to fight insurers, do it where it counts: on your own claims, at the ballot box, and by using Cost Plus Drugs and pharmacies like it when they're cheaper than your plan. That last one hits the insurer where Cuban actually hit them.

## The fight you can win

Medicare has an appeals process with deadlines and independent reviewers, and insurers count on you not using it. The numbers on Medicare Advantage denials are striking: plans deny millions of prior authorization requests a year, only a small share are appealed, and of those appealed, most are overturned. The denial is a bet that you'll give up.

**Original Medicare:** the denial appears on your Medicare Summary Notice. You have 120 days to request a redetermination. Five levels of appeal follow, and most disputes end at the first or second.

**Medicare Advantage:** 60 days from the denial notice to appeal. The plan must answer within 30 days for a service you haven't received, 60 for a payment dispute, and 72 hours if your doctor says waiting could harm you. If the plan says no again, the case automatically goes to an independent reviewer.

**Part D drug denials:** ask the plan for a coverage determination and, if your doctor supports it, an exception. If it's a "ghost approval" at an unaffordable tier, ask for a tiering exception in writing.

> **What to do:** Never accept the first no. Call the provider's billing office, get the reason code, and file the appeal before the deadline. Your State Health Insurance Assistance Program does this with you for free at 1-877-839-2675.

## What Cuban got right that nobody says

His pharmacy sells drugs at cost plus a fixed markup, and for a lot of generics it beats Medicare Part D copays outright. You can use it with no insurance at all. A retiree who checks Cost Plus for every generic before filling at the pharmacy counter is doing more damage to the insurance model than any 401(k) reshuffle ever will.

Fight the insurer on the claim. Keep the fund. And when a billionaire tells you your retirement account is the problem, ask what he's selling instead. In Cuban's case it's cheap drugs, and that part you should buy.
