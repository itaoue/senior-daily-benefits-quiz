A part-time job in the first years of retirement is common, and so is the surprise that follows: a letter from Social Security saying your benefit is being reduced because you earned too much. The rule behind that letter is called the retirement earnings test. It only applies before full retirement age, and the money it takes is not gone for good.

## The 2026 numbers

| Your situation in 2026 | Annual earnings limit | Reduction above the limit |
|---|---|---|
| Under full retirement age all year | $24,480 | $1 withheld for every $2 over |
| Reaching full retirement age during 2026 | $65,160 (counting only months before your birthday month) | $1 withheld for every $3 over |
| At or past full retirement age | No limit | None |

Full retirement age is 67 if you were born in 1960 or later, and between 66 and 67 for people born from 1955 through 1959.

An example: you're 64, collecting $1,800 a month, and you earn $34,480 from a job in 2026. That's $10,000 over the limit, so Social Security withholds $5,000 across the year. In practice it holds back whole monthly checks until the $5,000 is covered, then resumes payments.

> **What to do:** If you expect to earn more than the limit, tell Social Security now (call 1-800-772-1213 or use your my Social Security account). They'll adjust your payments in advance rather than sending an overpayment notice later.

## What counts, and what doesn't

Only earnings from work count: wages from a job and net profit from self-employment. The following do **not** count toward the limit:

- Pensions and annuity payments
- Withdrawals from IRAs and 401(k)s
- Investment income, interest, dividends, capital gains
- Rental income (unless you're in the business of managing property)
- Your spouse's earnings

There's also a monthly rule for your first year of retirement. If you retire mid-year and have already earned more than the annual limit, you can still get a full check for any month you earn under $2,040 (2026) and don't do substantial self-employment work.

> **What to do:** If you're leaving a job partway through the year, mention it when you apply. The monthly test often means a full benefit for the remaining months even if your January-to-June paycheck was large.

## The withheld money comes back

This is the part most people never hear. When you reach full retirement age, Social Security recalculates your benefit and credits you for the months it withheld. Your monthly check goes up permanently to make up for it. Over an average lifespan you get most of the withheld amount back, just spread out.

So the earnings test is closer to a forced delay than a penalty. Where it does real damage is when someone claims early, keeps working, and has most of their checks withheld anyway. In that case they took the permanent early-claiming reduction for benefits they mostly didn't receive.

## Congress may change this

A bipartisan bill to repeal the earnings test was introduced this year. Supporters say it discourages older people from working. Opponents point out that because the money is repaid later, repeal would mostly speed up payments rather than increase them, at a cost to the trust fund. It has not passed, and nothing changes for 2026.

> **What to do:** If you're under full retirement age, working, and haven't claimed yet, run the numbers before you apply. For many people with a steady paycheck, waiting until the job ends avoids both the earnings test and the early-claiming reduction.
