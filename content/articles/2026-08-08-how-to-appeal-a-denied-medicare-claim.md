A denial letter feels final. Usually it isn't. Many denials come down to a mismatched name, a wrong billing code, or a missing signature, and Medicare has a formal appeal process with deadlines that protect you as long as you act on time.

## First, find out why

Don't start with the appeal form. Start with the reason.

- **Original Medicare:** the denial appears on your Medicare Summary Notice (MSN), which arrives every three months. Each line shows what was billed, what Medicare paid, and a short reason code if it was denied.
- **Medicare Advantage or a Part D plan:** the plan sends its own denial notice with a reason and appeal instructions.

Then call the provider's billing office. A large share of denials are fixed by the office resubmitting the claim with corrected information. That's faster than an appeal and doesn't use up your appeal window.

> **What to do:** Check that the name and Medicare number on the claim match your red, white, and blue card exactly. Hyphenated, very long, or recently changed names cause more denials than most people realize. If the office has you listed differently, ask them to correct their records before resubmitting.

## Original Medicare: the five levels

1. **Redetermination.** Fill out the form on the back of your MSN (or write a letter) and send it to the address listed. **Deadline: 120 days** from the date on the MSN. You'll usually get a decision within 60 days.
2. **Reconsideration** by an independent contractor. Deadline: 180 days from the level 1 decision.
3. **Hearing before an administrative law judge.** Available if the amount in dispute meets a minimum (a few hundred dollars, adjusted yearly). Deadline: 60 days.
4. **Medicare Appeals Council review.** Deadline: 60 days.
5. **Federal court**, for larger amounts.

Most disputes end at level 1 or 2. Include a short note explaining why the service was medically necessary, and attach anything from your doctor that supports it.

> **What to do:** Write the appeal deadline on your calendar the day you receive the MSN. Missing it doesn't automatically end things (you can ask for an extension with good cause), but it makes everything harder.

## Medicare Advantage: faster, shorter deadlines

If your plan denies a service or payment, you have **60 days** from the denial notice to ask for a reconsideration. The plan must answer within 30 days for a service you haven't received yet, or 60 days for a payment dispute. If you or your doctor believes waiting could seriously harm your health, ask for an **expedited** appeal and the plan must decide within 72 hours.

If the plan upholds its denial, the case goes automatically to an independent reviewer. You don't have to file again.

## While the appeal is pending

You generally don't have to pay the disputed amount while the appeal is open, but the provider may still send bills. Tell them in writing that the claim is under Medicare appeal and ask them to hold collection. Keep copies of everything.

> **What to do:** If you'd like help, your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) offers free, one-on-one counseling on Medicare appeals. Find your local office at shiphelp.org or by calling 1-877-839-2675.
