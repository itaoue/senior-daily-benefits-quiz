In July, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent unveiled a one-dollar coin bearing Donald Trump's likeness to mark the 250th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence. He called it "a lasting symbol of patriotism" and "the enduring legacy of liberty." It's being struck in Philadelphia and goes on sale this fall.

Legal scholars say a living person's face on U.S. money breaks an 1866 statute, and a court declined to rule. We'll leave that fight to the lawyers. What matters for your money is simpler: the coin is not gold. Early renderings suggested it was. The finished version is base metal with a gold-colored finish, like every dollar coin since 2000. Its metal is worth pennies. Its face value is one dollar. It is, in the literal sense, fool's gold.

That's fine if you want one as a keepsake. Buy it from the Mint at the Mint's price. The trouble starts when the marketing around it blurs into the thing people actually mean when they say "gold."

## Why gold is in the ads this year

Gold has been near record highs, the national debt just crossed $40 trillion, and Bessent himself reminded a Fox News audience in July that the dollar hasn't been backed by gold since 1971. Every one of those facts is real, and every one is being cut into a commercial aimed at people over 60. Some gold in a retirement portfolio isn't crazy. Dalio holds it. Central banks are buying it. The question is never whether, it's how, and the "how" in the ads is the expensive way.

## Three things called "gold coins"

**Commemoratives** like the 250th-anniversary dollar. Keepsakes. Almost none ever trade above what you paid.

**Bullion coins**: American Gold Eagles, Canadian Maple Leafs. Worth the metal inside plus a small premium, usually a few percent. This is what "investing in gold" means.

**"Rare" or "proof" coins sold by phone and TV.** A bullion coin, graded and sealed in plastic, sold at two or three times its metal value on the theory that it's collectible. When you sell, it's worth the metal. The CFTC and state regulators list this among the top frauds against older Americans, and the coins are real. The price is the fraud.

> **What to do:** For any gold coin, divide the price by the ounces of gold inside and compare with the day's spot price. A premium over 10% needs an explanation. Over 30%, hang up.

## How to actually cash in, cheaply

- **A gold ETF in a regular brokerage or IRA account.** Tracks the metal's price, costs a fraction of a percent a year, sells in seconds. For most retirees who want gold exposure, this is the whole answer.
- **Bullion coins from a reputable dealer** if you want metal you can hold. Buy the common ones, pay a few percent over spot, store them somewhere insured.
- **A gold IRA** if you specifically want physical metal inside a retirement account. It's legal: approved coins and bars at a licensed depository through a custodian. Get the setup fee, annual custodian fee, storage fee, and the dealer's markup over spot in writing. Then compare the total with the ETF. The difference is what the ad is really selling.

> **What to do:** Keep gold to 5% to 10% of savings at most. It pays no interest or dividends, so it can't fund a monthly budget, and it fell 40% from 2011 to 2015 while stocks doubled. It's insurance, not income.

## The warning signs

A pitch that opens with the national debt or "what Washington doesn't want you to know." Pressure to move retirement money before a deadline. A refusal to quote the price per ounce over spot. A "guaranteed buyback." A request to wire money before paperwork arrives. Any two of those together is a scam, whatever it's selling.

The Trump coin will sell for a dollar and be worth a dollar. Real gold is worth real money. The people who profit most from confusing the two are counting on you not reading this far.
