Donald Trump once called himself "the king of debt," and he meant it as a compliment. On August 19 the federal government's debt passed $40 trillion, according to the Treasury's own daily tally. It was $30 trillion in early 2022. The 2025 tax law he signed adds $3.4 trillion to the pile over a decade by the Congressional Budget Office's estimate, more than $4 trillion once you count the interest on it.

The debt isn't a new problem or a one-party problem. But it stopped being an abstraction this summer. Interest on it now costs more than Medicare. And the week the total crossed $40 trillion, the 30-year Treasury yield touched 5.34%, the highest since 2007, because lenders want more to hold that much government paper. That number is the one that reaches into a retiree's life.

## Where the money goes

Through the first seven months of the fiscal year the Treasury paid $628 billion in net interest, more than it spent on Medicare. The CBO expects about $1 trillion in interest this year and $2.1 trillion within ten. That's money that pays for nothing, and when Washington goes looking for savings, the biggest line items are Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid. Nothing is decided. But that's the pressure behind every budget fight for the rest of your retirement.

> **What to do:** Plan for smaller cost-of-living adjustments, higher Medicare premiums, and slower fixes to Social Security's 2032 shortfall than the past few years would suggest. Benefit cuts for people already collecting remain unlikely. Squeezes around the edges are not.

## How it reaches your savings

**Your bond funds.** When yields rise, the price of bonds already issued falls. Long-term bond funds have shown it on statements all year. Check the "duration" figure on any fund you own: a duration of 12 means roughly a 12% price drop for each one-point rise in rates. Individual Treasuries held to maturity pay back in full; funds never mature.

**Your cash.** Here the debt works for you. The government paying more to borrow means banks, money market funds, and Treasury bills pay more too. A one-year T-bill or an online-bank CD now pays well above inflation for the first time in a generation.

**Your borrowing.** Mortgage rates follow the 10-year Treasury, which has also climbed. Home equity lines are variable, so the payment rises with rates. Car loans and credit cards follow the same current.

**Your purchasing power.** The historical way a heavily indebted government pays its bills is slow inflation, which taxes savers without a vote. That's the real long-run risk to a fixed income.

## Five moves that insulate a fixed income

1. **Move your reserve to where the yields are.** Treasury bills at TreasuryDirect, a high-yield savings account, or a CD ladder. This is the one place a $40 trillion debt is good news, and it takes fifteen minutes.
2. **Shorten your bonds.** Money you'll need within five years belongs in short-term funds or individual Treasuries, not a long-duration fund.
3. **Own inflation protection directly.** Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities and Series I bonds adjust with the CPI. They're the boring, correct hedge against the way this usually ends.
4. **Kill variable-rate debt.** A HELOC balance is the most expensive thing most retirees carry after credit cards, and it gets more expensive every time the Treasury pays more.
5. **Delay Social Security if you can.** It's the only inflation-indexed income that never runs out, and every year of delay between 62 and 70 buys more of it.

> **What to do:** Once a year, answer three questions. Is my cash earning close to the T-bill rate? Are my bonds short enough to sleep through rate moves? Is any variable-rate debt shrinking? Those three answers matter more to your retirement than the number on the debt clock.

## What the debt is not

It is not a reason to buy gold from a cable ad, crypto from a newsletter, or an annuity from a hotel-ballroom seminar. The pitches are loud because the fear is real. The dollar survived a bigger debt burden, relative to the economy, after World War II. Problems this size play out over decades, not overnight. The king of debt won't be paying this bill, but you can make sure your own savings are earning what the government is now forced to pay.
