A larger cost-of-living adjustment sounds like unqualified good news, and for people living on a fixed income it helps. But a COLA is a thermometer, not a bonus. It goes up because prices went up. Here's how to think about the one coming in January.

## Where the estimate comes from

Social Security's COLA is set by a formula, not a vote. It compares the average Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners (CPI-W) in July, August, and September with the same three months a year earlier. Whatever the percentage increase is, that's the raise.

Because two of those three months are already in, estimates get fairly accurate by late summer. The Senior Citizens League's August projection was 3.6%. The 2026 COLA was 2.8%, so if the estimate holds, next year's will be the largest since 2023.

The official number is announced in mid-October, when the September inflation report comes out. This year that's expected on October 14.

> **What to do:** Don't plan around 3.6% until it's official. The final figure can move a few tenths of a point in either direction depending on September's prices.

## What it means in dollars

The average retired worker's benefit is a little over $2,000 a month in 2026. A 3.6% increase on that is about $72 a month before deductions. On a $1,500 benefit it's about $54.

The new amount shows up in the January 2027 payment. For SSI recipients it arrives at the end of December, since SSI for January is paid early.

## The Part B catch

Most people have their Medicare Part B premium deducted from their Social Security check. The 2026 Part B premium is $202.90 a month, and the 2027 premium won't be announced until the fall. If Part B rises by, say, $15, that comes off the top of your COLA.

There is a protection called "hold harmless": if the Part B increase would be bigger than your COLA increase, your net check can't go down. But with a 3%-plus COLA, few people will hit that floor. Expect most of the raise to reach you, minus whatever Part B adds.

> **What to do:** When your COLA notice arrives in December (by mail or in your my Social Security account), compare the new gross benefit to the new net deposit. The difference is your 2027 Part B premium plus any Part D or Medicare Advantage premium you've chosen to have withheld.

## A bigger COLA can nudge your taxes

Whether Social Security is taxed depends on "combined income": your adjusted gross income, plus tax-exempt interest, plus half of your benefits. The thresholds ($25,000 single, $32,000 joint) have never been adjusted for inflation. So each COLA pushes a few more people over the line where up to 50% or 85% of benefits become taxable.

The new $6,000 senior deduction (through 2028) offsets this for many households, but it phases out at higher incomes.

> **What to do:** If your combined income sat just under $25,000 or $32,000 last year, a 3.6% raise could tip you over. Ask your preparer whether it's worth having federal tax withheld from your benefit (form W-4V) to avoid a surprise in April.

## What a COLA doesn't do

It doesn't restore ground lost in earlier years. The CPI-W tracks the spending of working households, which weights housing and gasoline heavily and medical care lightly. For retirees, whose health costs rise faster than the general index, a COLA tends to lag. That's a reason to treat the raise as a partial catch-up rather than extra spending money.
