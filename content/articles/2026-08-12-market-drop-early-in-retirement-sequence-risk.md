Robert Kiyosaki doesn't do subtle. The author of "Rich Dad Poor Dad," the best-selling personal finance book ever written, has spent years warning that the stock market, the bond market, and the dollar are headed for a fall. This summer he narrowed the target: baby boomers, he says, are being set up for a crash that will hit them at the worst possible moment, just as they start living off their savings.

He's vague on the date, as he usually is. But strip away the drama and there's a piece of arithmetic underneath that most retirement plans quietly ignore, and it hits very differently depending on how close you are to needing the money.

## The math Kiyosaki is pointing at

A market drop in your first few years of retirement does more damage than the same drop later. Not because the market is worse then. Because you're withdrawing while it's down, and the dollars you take out at the bottom never get to recover.

Two people retire with $500,000 and withdraw $25,000 a year. Both average the same return over 20 years. One gets the bad years first: down 20%, then down 15%, then recovery. The other gets the same bad years at the very end. The second person finishes far ahead, often by hundreds of thousands of dollars. The first may run short.

Planners call it sequence-of-returns risk. Younger investors don't have it, because they're adding money during downturns, not removing it. Retirees have it in full, and the years around your retirement date are when it's most dangerous. That's the part Kiyosaki has right, whatever you think of his timing.

> **What to do:** If you're within three years of retiring or already retired, and more than 70% of your savings is in stocks, you're exposed to exactly the scenario he's describing. The fixes are below, and none of them requires predicting a crash.

## Five ways to be ready whether or not he's right

**1. Keep two to three years of spending out of the market.** Cash, Treasury bills, CDs. When stocks fall, you spend this instead of selling. This single move neutralizes most of the risk, because the damage comes from selling low, not from the drop itself.

**2. Let Social Security carry the early years.** Each year you delay claiming between 62 and 70 raises the check about 7% to 8% for life. The less your first five years lean on the portfolio, the less a bad sequence can do.

**3. Flex the withdrawals.** Skip the inflation raise after a losing year. Trim by 5% to 10% if the balance falls below a line you set now. Plans with that rule survive bad sequences far more often than rigid ones.

**4. Rebalance on a calendar, not on a feeling.** Once or twice a year, sell what's up and buy what's down. Decide the rule while you're calm.

**5. Cover the essentials with guaranteed income.** Social Security, a pension, and for some people a plain immediate annuity for the gap. If rent, food, and insurance are paid no matter what stocks do, a crash becomes an inconvenience.

> **What to do:** Essential monthly bills minus guaranteed monthly income is what your portfolio must produce in a bad year. Multiply by 24. Make sure that amount is somewhere that can't fall.

## Where we part ways with him

Kiyosaki's prescription is to get out of paper assets and into gold, silver, and bitcoin. Some gold as insurance is reasonable. But none of those produce the monthly income a retiree lives on, silver is more volatile than the stock market, and bitcoin fell more than 70% in 2022. They're bets on a different sequence, not protection from any sequence.

Getting out of the market is easy. Getting back in is the part nobody times well, and missing a handful of the best days over a decade cuts returns roughly in half. The retiree who comes through the next downturn intact usually isn't the one who saw it coming. It's the one with two years of bills in Treasury bills, a benefit check that covers the rent, and a written rule for what to do when the balance drops.

Kiyosaki may be right this time. The plan above works either way. That's the point of a plan.
