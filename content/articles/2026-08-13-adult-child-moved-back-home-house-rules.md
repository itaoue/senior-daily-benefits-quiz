A grown child comes home for "a few months" after a breakup, a layoff, or a lease that got too expensive. The months become a year. They have a job. They don't contribute. And every conversation about it ends badly.

Financial coaches who work with families say the refusal is rarely about the money. It's usually embarrassment: paying rent to your parents makes the situation feel permanent. That doesn't change what it costs you.

## Start with your own numbers

Before any conversation, know what the arrangement actually costs. Groceries, utilities, car insurance if they're on your policy, the phone plan, streaming, and the little things. For most households it's several hundred dollars a month. Over a year on a retirement income, that's real money coming out of savings that was supposed to last.

> **What to do:** Write the monthly figure down. Not to present as a bill, but so you go into the conversation with a fact instead of a feeling.

## Make the contribution non-negotiable and the form flexible

The framework that tends to work: everyone who lives in the house contributes. That part isn't up for discussion. *How* they contribute can be.

- **Cash rent**, even a modest amount, paid on a fixed date like any other bill.
- **Specific expenses**, such as the grocery bill or the utilities, in their name.
- **A savings requirement.** Some parents take "rent" and put it in a separate account, returned when the child moves out as a deposit on their own place. The discipline is the point.
- **Labor**, if money is truly short: the deferred house projects, driving, yard work, on a schedule rather than "when I get to it."

Put it in writing, with a date to review it. A one-page agreement feels formal, and that's useful. It moves the arrangement from favor to plan.

> **What to do:** Set a move-out target together, even a loose one. "By next summer" changes the tone of every conversation that follows.

## Watch these details

**Taxes.** You can only claim an adult child as a dependent if their income is under a small threshold ($5,200 for 2025) and you provide more than half their support. A working adult child living at home almost never qualifies. If they file their own return, they should not be claimed on yours.

**Gifts.** Free housing isn't a taxable gift, and you can give anyone up to $19,000 a year (2025 and 2026) with no reporting at all. But if you're paying off their debts or handing over larger sums, keep track. Above the annual limit you file a gift-tax form, though no tax is usually owed.

**Your benefits.** If you receive SSI or a needs-based program, another adult's income in the household can affect your eligibility. Social Security retirement and Medicare are not affected.

**Insurance.** An adult child driving your car should be listed on your policy. An unlisted driver in an accident can void a claim.

## Don't sacrifice the thing that can't be replaced

Your child has decades to rebuild. You don't. Advisors are blunt on this point: if helping a child means drawing down retirement savings faster than planned, delaying Social Security you'd meant to delay, or carrying debt into your 70s, the help costs more than it gives. Setting terms isn't unkind. It's the version of help that leaves you both standing.
