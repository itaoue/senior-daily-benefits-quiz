Big changes to Social Security and retirement taxes usually arrive with a headline. These three didn't. They came through an agency notice, a bill signed during the holidays, and a rule buried in a 2022 law that only kicks in years later. If you're on Social Security or within ten years of it, all three touch your money.

## 1. Social Security can now withhold up to half your check to collect an overpayment

If Social Security decides it paid you too much in the past, even because of its own mistake, it can take the money back out of your future checks. For years the default withholding was 10% of your monthly benefit. In spring 2025 the agency briefly moved that default to 100%, then settled on **50%** for new retirement and disability overpayments.

That means a $2,000 check can become $1,000 until the balance is cleared, with no hearing beforehand.

> **What to do:** You don't have to accept the 50% rate. Call Social Security or file Form SSA-634 to ask for a lower monthly amount based on what you can afford. If the overpayment wasn't your fault and repaying it would be a hardship, file Form SSA-632 to ask for a waiver. Do this within 30 days of the overpayment notice to stop the withholding from starting.

## 2. Teachers, firefighters, and police officers got their full benefits back

Two rules called the Windfall Elimination Provision and the Government Pension Offset used to cut Social Security for people who also earned a pension from a job that didn't pay into the system. That covered about 3 million retired public employees and their spouses, some of whom lost hundreds of dollars a month.

The Social Security Fairness Act, signed in January 2025, repealed both rules and made the change retroactive to January 2024. Most affected people received a lump-sum back payment and a higher ongoing benefit during 2025.

> **What to do:** If you or your late spouse worked for a school district, city, or state agency and you've never applied for Social Security because you assumed it would be reduced to nothing, apply now. Widows and widowers of public employees are the group most likely to still be missing out.

## 3. The age when the IRS forces you to withdraw from your IRA is moving to 75

Required minimum distributions, or RMDs, are the withdrawals the IRS makes you take from traditional IRAs and 401(k)s each year once you reach a certain age. Every dollar you take out is taxed as income, and skipping one used to cost a 50% penalty.

The SECURE 2.0 Act changed both parts:

- If you were born between 1951 and 1959, your RMDs start at **73**.
- If you were born in 1960 or later, they start at **75**.
- The penalty for missing an RMD dropped to 25%, and to 10% if you fix it quickly.

> **What to do:** More years before RMDs means more years to move money from a traditional IRA into a Roth IRA in small, low-tax chunks. Those conversions are taxed now but grow tax-free afterward and are never subject to RMDs. If your income is lower in the years between retiring and 73 or 75, that window is when conversions cost the least. Run the numbers with a tax preparer before December, since a conversion has to be done in the calendar year it counts for.

## One more that helps most people over 65

Starting with 2025 tax returns, people 65 and older get an extra **$6,000 deduction** ($12,000 for a married couple who are both 65+) on top of the standard deduction, through tax year 2028. It phases out for single filers above $75,000 of income and couples above $150,000. It's automatic when you file, but it's another reason to keep your taxable income in check during those Roth conversion years.

None of these changes require you to be a tax expert. They require you to know they happened, which is more than most people do.
