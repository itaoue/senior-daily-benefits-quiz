Somebody in a newsroom named the 2027 Social Security increase the "Trump Bump," and it stuck. The Senior Citizens League's August estimate has the cost-of-living adjustment at about 3.6%, up from 2.8% this year and the largest since 2023. On the average retired worker's check, that's roughly $72 a month starting in January.

On the surface, good news. Here's the catch. A COLA rises because prices rose. Consumer prices are up 3.4% over the past year according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and that's why the adjustment is big. Retirees are the one group in America with an automatic inflation adjustment written into law. Everything else they own, the savings account, the CD, the emergency fund, the cash in the drawer, is exposed to the exact same forces driving the bump, with no adjustment at all.

## What the bump is actually worth

Social Security compares the average CPI-W for July, August, and September with the same months a year earlier. That percentage is the raise, announced with the September inflation report, expected October 14.

Then the deductions start. Medicare Part B is $202.90 a month in 2026 and the 2027 premium comes out this fall; health costs run ahead of general inflation, so a $12 to $18 increase wouldn't surprise anyone. Stand-alone drug plan premiums are likely to rise more than usual in 2027 because the federal subsidy that held them down is ending. Call it $45 to $55 of the $72 that actually lands in your account. Against grocery, insurance, and utility bills up 3.4%.

> **What to do:** Don't plan around 3.6% until October. When the COLA notice arrives in December, compare the new gross benefit to the new net deposit. The gap is Medicare's share.

## The tax trap that comes with a bigger COLA

The income thresholds that decide whether your benefits are taxed, $25,000 for a single filer and $32,000 for a couple, were set in 1983 and have never been indexed. Every COLA pushes more retirees over them. A 3.6% raise will tip some people into having up to 50% or 85% of benefits taxed for the first time. The $6,000 senior deduction offsets that for many households, through 2028.

> **What to do:** If your combined income last year was within a few thousand dollars of $25,000 or $32,000, ask your preparer about withholding federal tax from your benefit with form W-4V.

## The real catch: everything that isn't Social Security

If 3.4% inflation makes your benefit go up 3.6%, you roughly broke even on that income. The savings account paying 0.5% lost about 3%. The five-year CD you bought in 2021 at 1% has been losing ground every year since. Cash under the mattress lost the full 3.4%. Retirees with $100,000 in low-yield cash lost roughly $3,000 of purchasing power this year and never saw it on a statement.

That is what the bump is compensating for on one part of your income, and what nothing is compensating for on the rest.

## How to guard the rest

- **Series I savings bonds.** The rate is set by the same CPI that sets your COLA. Up to $10,000 a year per person at TreasuryDirect, no state tax.
- **Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS).** Same idea for larger amounts, in a brokerage account or a low-cost fund.
- **Treasury bills and high-yield savings** for the cash reserve. They've been paying above inflation, unlike the branch bank downtown.
- **Certificates of deposit** if you'd rather lock in a rate. Compare online banks and credit unions; the spread between the best and worst CD rates is often two full percentage points.
- **Stocks**, over the long run, because companies raise prices along with everyone else. A broad index fund has beaten inflation in every 20-year stretch on record.
- **A little gold or real estate**, if you want it, through a low-cost fund rather than a phone salesman.

> **What to do:** Total your cash and CDs. If more than three years of spending is sitting somewhere earning less than the current inflation rate, that's the part of your money the Trump Bump can't help. Move it this fall, before the bump arrives, so both halves of your income are keeping up.
