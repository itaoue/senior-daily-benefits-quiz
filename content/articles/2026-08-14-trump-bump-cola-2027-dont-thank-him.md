Somebody in a newsroom came up with "Trump Bump" for the 2027 Social Security increase, and it stuck. The Senior Citizens League's August estimate has the cost-of-living adjustment at about 3.6%, up from 2.8% this year and the largest since 2023. Around $72 a month on an average check.

Here's the thing about a cost-of-living adjustment. It's set by a formula written in 1972. It rises when the Consumer Price Index rises. It has nothing to do with who's in the White House, except in one way: whoever's policies push prices up gets a bigger COLA named after them. That's not a compliment. It's an invoice.

## Where the 3.6% comes from

Social Security compares the average CPI-W for July, August, and September to the same three months a year earlier. That percentage is the raise. The official number comes out with the September inflation report, expected October 14.

Consumer prices rose 3.4% over the year to July, per the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Economists at Yale's Budget Lab and elsewhere have estimated that the tariffs imposed since 2025 account for somewhere around three-quarters of a percentage point to over a point of that, depending on how much of the cost importers pass along. Take the tariffs out and you're looking at a COLA closer to 2.5%. So roughly a quarter to a third of the "bump" is a refund for a price increase the administration chose.

> **What to do:** Don't plan around 3.6% until the October announcement. The final figure can move a few tenths either way based on September prices.

## What you actually keep

Most retirees have their Medicare Part B premium deducted from the check. Part B is $202.90 a month in 2026. The 2027 premium is announced in the fall, and with health costs running ahead of general inflation, a $12 to $18 increase wouldn't surprise anyone. That comes straight off the top of the COLA.

On an average check: about $72 more, minus maybe $15 for Part B, minus any increase in your Part D or Medicare Advantage premium, which is likely to be bigger than usual next year because the government ended the subsidy that held stand-alone drug plan premiums down. Call it $45 to $55 in your pocket. Against grocery, insurance, and utility bills that have risen 3.4%.

> **What to do:** When the COLA notice arrives in December, compare the new gross benefit to the new net deposit. The gap is what Medicare took. If it ate more than half the raise, you're not imagining it.

## The tax trap nobody names

The income thresholds that decide whether your Social Security is taxed, $25,000 for a single filer and $32,000 for a couple, were set in 1983 and have never been adjusted for inflation. Every COLA pushes more retirees over them. A 3.6% increase will tip some people into having up to 50% or 85% of benefits taxed for the first time. The senior deduction offsets that for many, for four years. Then it expires.

> **What to do:** If your combined income last year was within a few thousand dollars of $25,000 or $32,000, ask your preparer about withholding federal tax from your benefit with form W-4V. It beats a surprise bill in April.

## Why the name matters

A COLA is the program working as designed. It's the one part of Social Security that doesn't need a hero, and calling it a "bump" gives credit for a paycheck to the person who inflated the prices it's chasing. The same administration whose tariffs are in that 3.4% is the one whose tax law took $169 billion out of the trust fund that pays the COLA. If you want to thank someone for next year's raise, thank the 1972 Congress that indexed benefits, and then ask the current one what it plans to do about 2032.
