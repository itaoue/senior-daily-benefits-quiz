Decades of saving train people not to touch the money. That instinct serves you well until the year you turn 73, when the IRS begins requiring withdrawals from traditional IRAs and 401(k)s whether you need the income or not. The withdrawal is taxed as ordinary income, and the required percentage grows every year.

## The basics

- **Who:** anyone with a traditional IRA, SEP or SIMPLE IRA, 401(k), 403(b), or similar pre-tax account. Roth IRAs have no RMD during the owner's lifetime, and since 2024 neither do Roth 401(k)s.
- **When:** the year you turn 73 if you were born from 1951 through 1959. If you were born in 1960 or later, the starting age is 75.
- **How much:** your account balance on December 31 of the previous year, divided by a life-expectancy factor from the IRS table. At 73 the factor is 26.5, so the withdrawal is about 3.8% of the balance. At 80 it's about 5%. At 90, more than 8%.
- **Deadline:** December 31 each year. For your very first RMD only, you can wait until April 1 of the following year, but then you'll take two in one year, which can push you into a higher bracket.
- **Penalty for missing it:** 25% of the amount you should have withdrawn, reduced to 10% if you correct it within two years.

> **What to do:** If you have several IRAs, you can take the total RMD from any one of them. 401(k)s are different: each plan's RMD must come from that plan. Consolidating old 401(k)s into one IRA before 73 makes this simpler.

## Why waiting can backfire

The forced withdrawal doesn't just create taxable income. It can:

- push more of your Social Security into the taxable range,
- trigger higher Medicare Part B and D premiums two years later (the IRMAA surcharge starts at $109,000 of income for a single filer in 2026, $218,000 for a couple), and
- eat into the new $6,000 senior deduction, which phases out above $75,000 of income for singles and $150,000 for couples.

A large traditional balance that's never been touched is a tax bill waiting to happen, and the bill arrives on the IRS's schedule.

## Three moves for your 60s

**1. Fill the low brackets with Roth conversions.** Between retirement and 73 many people have a stretch of low-income years. Converting part of a traditional IRA to a Roth each year, up to the top of the 12% or 22% bracket, moves money out of the RMD system permanently. You pay tax now at a rate you choose rather than later at a rate you don't. Conversions can't be undone, so size them carefully and mind the IRMAA thresholds.

**2. Start withdrawing before you have to.** Taking modest distributions in your 60s, even if you just move the money to a taxable account, shrinks the balance the RMD formula will be applied to. Spreading income over more years usually means less total tax.

**3. Give directly from the IRA if you give to charity.** Once you're 70½ you can send money straight from an IRA to a charity as a qualified charitable distribution. It counts toward your RMD once RMDs begin, it never shows up in your income, and you don't need to itemize. The annual limit is indexed, $108,000 per person for 2025.

> **What to do:** Pull your most recent statements and add up every pre-tax balance. Divide by 26.5. That's roughly your first RMD at today's balances, before any growth. If the number surprises you, that's the signal to talk to a preparer about conversions this year rather than in the year you turn 72.

## If you're still working at 73

If you're employed and don't own more than 5% of the company, you can delay RMDs from that employer's 401(k) until you retire. This doesn't apply to IRAs or to old 401(k)s from previous jobs.
