When a death notice runs in the paper or online, it usually lists the surviving spouse, the children, and the grandchildren by name, along with the town. To a grieving family that's a tribute. To a scammer it's a target list with a script attached.

Here are the four patterns showing up most often this year, and then the habits that beat all of them.

## Pattern 1: The "grandchild in trouble" call

Someone calls in the middle of the night. It's your grandson, or someone claiming to be a lawyer or police officer with him. He's been in an accident or arrested, he's scared, and he needs bail or hospital money right now. Please don't tell his parents.

The new twist: the voice on the line may actually sound like your grandchild. A few seconds of audio from a social media video is enough to clone a voice.

## Pattern 2: The debt that doesn't exist

A caller says your late husband owed money, on a credit card, a medical bill, a loan, and it's now your responsibility. Sometimes they have his real account numbers, pulled from a data breach. In most cases you are not personally responsible for a deceased spouse's individual debts; those are handled by the estate.

## Pattern 3: The "benefits" caller

Someone from "Social Security" or "Medicare" needs to update your record after your spouse's death, and to do that they need your Social Security number, your Medicare number, or your bank details. The real agencies already have this information. They will never call to ask for it.

## Pattern 4: The mortgage and title trick

A letter or call offers to help with the house now that you're on your own: refinance, reverse mortgage, or "reassessment." Some are aggressive sales. Some are outright fraud, including deed theft, where a forged document transfers your home's title without your knowledge.

## The three habits

> **Habit 1: Hang up and call back on a number you already have.** Not the number the caller gives you. The number on the back of your Medicare card, the number in your address book for your grandson, the number on your bank statement. Every legitimate caller will understand. Every scammer will try to keep you on the line.

> **Habit 2: Never pay with gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency, or cash handed to a courier.** No government agency, hospital, court, or utility accepts payment that way. The moment you hear it, the call is a scam. This one rule stops the majority of losses.

> **Habit 3: Agree on a family password.** Pick a word only your immediate family knows. If a caller claims to be a relative in trouble, ask for the word. A cloned voice can't answer.

## If it already happened

Call your bank immediately; transfers can sometimes be reversed within hours. Report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and, for amounts over a few thousand dollars, at IC3.gov, the FBI's fraud portal. Then tell someone. Shame keeps most victims quiet, and silence is what lets the same crew call the next name on the list.
