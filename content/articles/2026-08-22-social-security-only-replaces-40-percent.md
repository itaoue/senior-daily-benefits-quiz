Dave Ramsey has spent 30 years on the radio telling people to get out of debt, and he's blunt about the retirement version of the same problem. The biggest Social Security mistake, he says, has nothing to do with when you claim or how the trust fund is doing. It's treating the check as the plan.

Surveys keep finding that somewhere around half of working Americans expect Social Security to be their main source of income in retirement. The Social Security Administration's own numbers say it replaces roughly **40% of pre-retirement income** for an average earner, less for higher earners, a bit more for lower ones. It was designed in 1935 as a floor, with a pension and savings on top. The pensions are gone. For a lot of households, nothing took their place.

Ramsey's point is that the gap between "40%" and "my whole plan" is where retirements go wrong, and that the fix starts with admitting the gap exists. He's right, and his three moves work at 45 or at 64.

## Step 1: Get the real number, then stop guessing

Ramsey's first instruction is to know exactly what the check will be, because a number you've never looked at can't anchor a plan. Log in at ssa.gov/myaccount and open your Social Security Statement. It shows the estimated benefit at 62, at full retirement age, and at 70. Most people have never seen those three figures side by side, and the spread between them is usually the biggest lever left in their financial life.

While you're there, check the earnings record. A missing year, common for people who changed names or worked for an employer that misfiled, permanently lowers the benefit. SSA will correct it if you have a W-2 or a tax return.

> **What to do:** Write your full-retirement-age benefit on one line and your essential monthly bills on the next. The difference is the gap Ramsey is talking about. Everything below is about closing it.

## Step 2: Treat the claiming date as math, not a mood

Claiming at 62 instead of full retirement age (66 or 67, depending on birth year) permanently cuts the check by up to 30%. Waiting from full retirement age to 70 raises it 8% a year. For a $2,000 full-age benefit, that's about $1,400 a month at 62 versus $2,480 at 70, for life, with cost-of-living adjustments on top.

Ramsey's advice, and ours: if you're still working or can bridge a few years from savings, delaying is the highest-return move available to a retiree, and it makes the 40% bigger for good. If you're in poor health or have no other income, claiming earlier can be right. Married couples should look at the higher earner delaying, because that benefit becomes the survivor benefit for whoever lives longer.

## Step 3: Shrink what the check has to cover

This is where Ramsey's debt message and the Social Security problem meet. A benefit that covers 40% of your old paycheck covers a lot more of your bills if the bills are smaller, and the biggest ones are the ones people carry into retirement out of habit.

- **Debt first.** A car payment or a card balance is the single largest fixed cost a retiree can eliminate, and Ramsey would tell you to do it before the paychecks stop, not after.
- **Housing.** Senior property tax exemptions exist in nearly every state and are worth $500 to $2,000 a year. Most take one application at the county assessor.
- **Medicare.** Medicare Savings Programs pay the Part B premium, $202.90 a month in 2026, for people under set income limits. That's more than $2,400 a year back in the check.
- **Phone, utilities, everything else.** Lifeline, 55+ plans, and the senior discount you have to ask for.

## The starting assumption

Ramsey's version is simple: Social Security is a supplement, and everything else, savings, a paid-off house, no debt, is the plan. That's harder to hear at 60 than at 30. But the three steps above are the ones that still work late, and half the country hasn't taken the first one. Take it this week.
