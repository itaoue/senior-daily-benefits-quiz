"We will not touch Social Security." Donald Trump said it in 2016, in 2020, and throughout 2024. It was the one line seniors of both parties could count on hearing.

In July 2025 he signed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. It didn't cut anyone's check. What it did was quieter. It reduced the federal income tax that higher-income retirees pay on their Social Security benefits, mostly through the new $6,000 senior deduction. That tax isn't general revenue. Under a 1983 law, it flows straight into the Social Security trust fund. Less of it means less money in the fund.

The Social Security Administration's chief actuary, Karen Glenn, did the math in a letter to Senator Ron Wyden. Net cost to the program over 2025 through 2034: $168.6 billion. Effect on the retirement trust fund's depletion date: moved from early 2033 to the fourth quarter of 2032. The 2026 Trustees Report, released in June, confirmed the new date.

He said he wouldn't touch it. He touched it. The people who told you otherwise were reading the press release, not the actuary's letter.

## What "depletion" means for your check

When the trust fund's reserve hits zero, Social Security doesn't stop. Payroll taxes keep coming in and cover about 78% of promised benefits. Under current law, that's what gets paid. On the average retired worker's check of roughly $2,000, a 22% cut is about $440 a month, gone, for current retirees and future ones alike.

That was going to happen in 2033 anyway, on the previous administration's watch and every administration before it. Nobody in either party has fixed it in 40 years. But "I made an existing problem one quarter worse while promising to make it better" is not the same as "I didn't touch it," and we think seniors are owed the difference.

> **What to do:** Sign in to your my Social Security account and write down your projected benefit. Multiply it by 0.78. Build the budget on the second number. If Congress acts, the difference is a bonus. If it doesn't, you're already ready.

## The $6,000 deduction, honestly

We've praised the senior deduction on this site and we still do. For a retiree in the 12% bracket it's worth about $720 a year, and for a couple in the 22% bracket about $2,600. It's real money and you should take it.

But it was sold as "no tax on Social Security," which it isn't, and it was paid for partly by Social Security itself. Retirees are being handed a four-year tax break financed by a permanent hole in their own program. The deduction expires after 2028. The $169 billion doesn't come back.

## What the same bill did to Medicare and Medicaid

The Congressional Budget Office scored the law at $3.4 trillion in added deficits over ten years. To offset part of that, it cut Medicaid by about $1 trillion. Thirteen million older and disabled Americans are on both Medicare and Medicaid, and Medicaid pays for most nursing home care in the country. The law also triggered budget rules that call for roughly $500 billion in automatic Medicare cuts unless Congress waives them.

"We will not touch Social Security" was the promise. Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid were all touched, in the same bill, by the same signature.

> **What to do:** If you or a spouse could ever need Medicaid for long-term care, talk to an elder-law attorney now about how the state's rules are changing. The cuts phase in over several years and the planning window is open.

## What would actually fix it

Every serious proposal uses the same levers: raise or remove the $184,500 cap on wages subject to payroll tax, nudge the rate up, slowly raise the retirement age for people now in their 40s, trim the formula for the highest earners. In 1983, a Republican president and a Democratic speaker did a version of that a few months before the deadline. It can be done. It requires a president willing to say the word "Social Security" in a sentence that isn't a promise.

> **What to do:** Both of your senators and your representative have a position on the 2032 date, whether they've said it out loud or not. Ask them, in writing, which lever they'd pull. A retiree who asks that question is worth more to a campaign than a rally crowd. They know it. Make them prove it.
