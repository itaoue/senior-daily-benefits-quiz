Within weeks of a death, the calls start. A collector says your mother owed $9,000 on a card and asks how you'd like to take care of it. They sound sympathetic. They also know that most people in that moment will reach for a checkbook rather than ask a question.

Here's the question: **Was I a co-signer or joint account holder?** If not, the answer to "how would you like to pay" is "I'm not responsible for this debt, and please send everything in writing to the estate."

## The rule

When someone dies, their debts are paid from their **estate**: the money and property they left behind, before anything is distributed to heirs. If the estate has enough, the debts get paid. If it doesn't, the creditors take what there is and the rest is written off. Family members are not personally on the hook.

There are exceptions, and they're specific:

- You **co-signed** the loan or card, or were a **joint account holder** (not an authorized user; authorized users owe nothing).
- You live in a **community property state** (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, Wisconsin) and the debt was your spouse's. Even then, the rules are complex and often limit what a surviving spouse owes.
- State law makes a spouse responsible for certain **medical bills**. This varies; ask a local attorney or Legal Aid before paying.
- You're the **executor** and you paid heirs before paying valid creditors. Executors can be personally liable for distributing money that should have gone to debts, so pay in the right order.

## What collectors can and can't do

Under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, a collector may contact the executor or administrator of the estate. They may not tell you that you owe the debt if you don't. They may not threaten, imply legal action that isn't planned, or call repeatedly. If a collector says "you're responsible now," ask them to put that claim in writing. Most will not, because it's false.

> **What to do:** Send one letter, by mail with tracking, to each collector: the person's name and date of death, a statement that you are not personally liable, and the name of the executor if there is one. Keep copies. After that, you don't have to take their calls.

## The house, and the tenants

If there's a house, it's part of the estate and mortgage payments still need to be made, or the lender can foreclose. If you inherit it, you can generally keep paying the existing mortgage under federal rules that protect heirs. If the house has tenants, their lease survives the owner's death; you become the landlord and have to follow state notice rules before asking anyone to leave. Don't change the locks.

## Watch for this

Scammers read obituaries and call families claiming debts that don't exist. Any collector who wants payment by gift card, wire, or crypto, or who won't send documentation, is not a real creditor. And be careful of "debt relief" companies that show up in this window; some are legitimate, some charge large fees to negotiate debts the family didn't owe in the first place.

Grief is not a reason to pay something you don't owe. Ask the question, get it in writing, and take your time.
