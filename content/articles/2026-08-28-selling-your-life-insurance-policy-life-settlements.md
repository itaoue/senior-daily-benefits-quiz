A permanent life insurance policy bought decades ago to protect a young family can become a burden in retirement: the premiums keep coming, and the people it was meant to protect are grown. Surrendering it to the insurer returns the cash value, which is often small. A life settlement company will sometimes offer several times that amount.

The reason is simple. The buyer is betting on your lifespan. They pay you a lump sum, take over the premiums, and receive the full death benefit when you die. The sooner that happens, the better their return. Understanding that is the first step to deciding whether the trade is a good one for you.

## How it works

- Typically you need to be 65 or older with a policy face value of $100,000 or more. Universal, whole, and convertible term policies qualify.
- A broker or provider reviews your medical records and the policy, then makes an offer. Offers vary widely. Shopping more than one is essential.
- If you accept, ownership and the beneficiary designation transfer to the buyer. Your family receives nothing from the policy when you die.
- Most states license and regulate life settlement providers and brokers. Check with your state insurance department before signing anything.

## What you give up

**The death benefit.** Obvious, but worth saying plainly: your heirs lose it. If any part of your estate plan counted on it, the plan needs redoing.

**Tax.** The proceeds are not tax-free. Roughly, the amount up to what you paid in premiums comes back tax-free, the portion up to the policy's cash value is taxed as ordinary income, and anything above that is a capital gain. A large settlement can create a large tax bill in one year and push up Medicare premiums two years later.

**Means-tested benefits.** A lump sum counts as an asset. If you receive Medicaid, SSI, or a state assistance program, a settlement can make you ineligible until the money is spent down.

**Privacy.** The buyer, and sometimes investors the policy is resold to, will periodically check on your health. That's part of the arrangement.

> **What to do:** Before requesting an offer, ask a tax preparer to estimate the tax on a hypothetical settlement, and if you receive any needs-based benefit, ask the program how a lump sum would be treated.

## Check these alternatives first

1. **Accelerated death benefit.** Many policies let you draw part of the death benefit while alive if you're terminally or chronically ill. Usually tax-free, no sale involved.
2. **Reduced paid-up insurance.** Stop paying premiums and keep a smaller death benefit for life. No cash out, but no more cost.
3. **A policy loan.** Borrow against the cash value. Interest accrues, and the unpaid balance reduces the death benefit, but the policy stays in the family.
4. **Have a family member take over the premiums** in exchange for being named beneficiary. Often the best outcome when the policy is large and the premiums are the only problem.
5. **Surrender**, if the cash value is meaningful and the tax is small.

> **What to do:** Call your insurer and ask for an "in-force illustration" plus a list of non-forfeiture options. That one document shows what the policy is worth to you today under every choice except selling it.

## If you do sell

- Get offers from at least two or three licensed providers. Ask each broker what commission they're paid and by whom.
- Ask for the offer net of all fees, in writing.
- Confirm your state's rescission period, usually 15 to 30 days after closing, during which you can cancel.
- Tell your family. A settlement discovered after a death, when the expected benefit doesn't arrive, is the kind of surprise that turns grief into anger.
