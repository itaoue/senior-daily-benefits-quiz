Downsizing works for a lot of retirees. A smaller place means lower taxes, lower utilities, less maintenance, and, ideally, a pile of cash from the difference. But the math that looks clean on a napkin gets messy in practice. Here are the costs that surprise people, roughly in the order they show up.

## 1. Selling costs: 6 to 8% of the price, gone

Agent commissions have come down since the 2024 rule changes but still commonly run 4 to 5% total, plus title, escrow, transfer taxes, and the repairs a buyer's inspector will demand. On a $450,000 home, expect $30,000 to $35,000 to disappear before you see a dime.

## 2. Capital gains tax on the appreciation

If you've owned and lived in the home for at least two of the last five years, you can exclude **$250,000** of gain from tax if single, **$500,000** if married. Anything above that is taxed. A couple who bought for $120,000 in 1990 and sells for $700,000 has a $580,000 gain; $80,000 of it is taxable. Keep records of every improvement you've made over the years; each one raises your cost basis and shrinks the taxable gain.

## 3. The new place costs more per square foot

Smaller homes, especially newer condos and 55+ communities, often sell at a higher price per square foot than the old house. Half the space does not mean half the price. Budget from real listings, not assumptions.

## 4. HOA and community fees

Condo and 55+ community fees of $300 to $800 a month are common, and they rise. Ask for the last three years of fee history and the reserve study. A poorly funded reserve means a special assessment is coming, and those can run into five figures.

## 5. Property tax reset

In many states your property tax is based on the price you paid years ago. Buy a new place and it resets to today's value. Some states let seniors carry their old assessment to a new home; California's Proposition 19 does, for people 55 and older, up to three times. Check your state's rules before you sell, not after.

## 6. Moving, furnishing, and the "small stuff"

Professional movers for a long-distance move run $5,000 to $15,000. Then the old furniture doesn't fit, the new kitchen needs a different fridge, and the first year of a new house always has surprises: a water heater, a roof section, a fence. A home warranty for the first year or two can cap some of that.

## 7. Medicare premiums can jump

A large capital gain shows up as income on your tax return, and two years later Medicare uses that return to set your Part B and Part D premiums. One big year can push you into a higher bracket (IRMAA) and cost an extra $70 to $400 a month for a year. You can appeal if the sale was a one-time event, using form SSA-44.

> **What to do:** Before listing, run the full number: sale price, minus selling costs, minus tax, minus the new home, minus moving and setup, minus a year of the new fees. If what's left is meaningfully more than staying put and fixing what needs fixing, downsize. If it's close, staying and using a home equity line, or simply making the house easier to live in, is often the better financial move.

One more option that gets overlooked: renting the old house out and renting something smaller yourself. It keeps the appreciation, avoids the tax hit for now, and can be reversed. It's not for everyone, but it belongs on the list.
