"How much do I need?" gets answered with a big round number, and the number keeps climbing. But a savings target on its own tells you very little. A retiree with $750,000, a paid-off house, and two Social Security checks is in better shape than one with $1.5 million, a mortgage, and one check. Here's how to work out where you stand.

## Number one: what your savings can pay

The common rule of thumb is that you can withdraw about 4% of your savings in the first year and raise it with inflation each year after, with a good chance of the money lasting 30 years. On $750,000 that's **$30,000 a year**, or $2,500 a month.

Some planners argue for 3.5% in expensive markets, others for 5% if you're willing to cut back in bad years. But 4% is a reasonable place to start, and it tells you something important: a nest egg that sounds large produces a fairly modest income.

## Number two: what Social Security adds

The average retired worker's benefit in 2026 is a little over $2,000 a month. For a couple with two work histories, a combined $3,500 to $4,500 a month is common. That's $42,000 to $54,000 a year, adjusted for inflation every year, for life.

Put the two together and a couple with $750,000 and typical benefits has **$72,000 to $84,000 a year** before tax. A single person with an average benefit and the same savings has about $54,000.

> **What to do:** Get your real number, not the average. Sign in at ssa.gov/myaccount and read the estimate for the age you actually plan to claim. If you're married, do both.

## Number three: what you spend

Households headed by someone 65 or older spend about $60,000 a year on average, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Housing is the biggest piece, then transportation, food, and health care. The average hides enormous variation: a paid-off house in a low-tax state and a mortgage in a coastal city are different lives at the same income.

> **What to do:** Take your last three months of bank and credit-card statements and total them. Multiply by four. That's your real spending, and it beats any survey.

## Putting it together

If income from step one and two covers spending from step three with room to spare, $750,000 is enough. If it's close, the gap is what to work on. And the levers that close it are more powerful than most people expect:

- **Claiming Social Security later.** Each year you wait between 62 and 70 adds roughly 7% to 8% to the check, permanently and inflation-adjusted. Delaying from 65 to 68 on a $2,000 benefit adds about $480 a month for life. No investment offers that reliably.
- **Eliminating debt before you retire.** A $1,200 mortgage payment is the same as needing $360,000 more in savings at a 4% withdrawal rate.
- **Location.** State income tax on retirement income ranges from zero to nearly 10%, and property taxes vary even more.
- **Part-time work in the early years**, which lets the savings keep growing untouched.

## What not to do

Don't sell things you can't buy back. Advisors see retirees liquidate a pension's lump-sum option, an old whole-life policy, or a low-rate mortgage's flexibility because a spreadsheet said the number looked better. Guaranteed income, tax-advantaged accounts, and a home you own outright are the assets that make $750,000 work. Keep them.

> **What to do:** Write your three numbers on one page: safe withdrawal, Social Security, spending. If the first two are less than the third, pick one lever from the list above and work on it before you retire, not after.
