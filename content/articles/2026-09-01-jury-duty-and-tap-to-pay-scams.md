## Scam 1: "You missed jury duty and there's a warrant"

The call comes from someone identifying as a sheriff's deputy, a court officer, or a U.S. Marshal. They have your name and address. They say you failed to appear for jury duty, a judge has issued a warrant, and you can either come in to be arrested or clear it right now by paying a fine. The fine has to be paid today, and it has to be paid in a way that "the court system accepts": gift cards, a cryptocurrency ATM, a wire, or a payment app.

Everything about it is designed to make you act before you think. The caller ID often shows a real courthouse number, spoofed. Some callers stay on the line while you drive to the store, coaching you through buying the cards.

Here's the fact that ends every version of this scam: **courts never call to demand payment, and no government office accepts gift cards or crypto.** A real jury summons comes by mail. A real warrant is not something you can pay off over the phone.

> **What to do:** Hang up. If you're worried, look up your county court's number yourself and call it. Never call back the number they gave you. Then report the call at ReportFraud.ftc.gov; it takes two minutes and the reports are how these rings get traced.

## Scam 2: Tap-to-pay theft in crowds

Contactless cards and phones can be charged by tapping them against a payment terminal. Thieves have figured out that a handheld terminal, set to a small amount and held against a purse or back pocket in a crowded aisle, can pull a payment without the cardholder ever noticing. Reports have come from grocery stores, festivals, and transit. The amounts are small, usually under $100, which means many victims don't spot them for weeks.

There's a second version that involves a person, not a device: a "cashier" or helper asks you to tap your card or phone to "verify" or "check the balance," and instead runs a charge or, on a phone, initiates a transfer.

> **What to do:** Turn on transaction alerts for every card and bank app so you get a text for every charge, no matter how small. That single setting catches this scam the moment it happens. If you'd rather not fuss with settings, a $5 RFID-blocking sleeve or wallet physically prevents the tap. And never hand your phone or tap it on someone else's device to "verify" anything.

## If money already left

Call the number on the back of your card immediately and dispute the charge. Card issuers reverse most fraudulent contactless charges without argument. For a payment made under pressure, gift cards or crypto, contact the gift card company or the ATM operator right away; recovery is rare but faster is better. Then report it, and tell someone in your family. The next call is coming, and the person who knows the pattern is the person who hangs up.
