Dave Ramsey has said it on the radio for 30 years, and his company still says it: Social Security is "barely enough to keep the lights on and food on the table." Plenty of retirees find that insulting. They paid into the system for 45 years and would like to hear that it counts for something.

It does count. It just doesn't count for as much as most people assume, and this week Money Talks News did the useful thing of checking Ramsey's line against the numbers. We think the check comes out in his favor.

## What the check actually pays

The average retired worker's benefit was $2,086 a month in July, according to Social Security's own snapshot. That's about $25,000 a year. Median household spending for people over 65 runs around $60,000. Even a couple with two average benefits comes in under that, before health costs, which rise faster than the check does.

The program was designed that way. It replaces roughly 40% of an average earner's pre-retirement income, on the theory that a pension and savings would cover the rest. The pension part of that theory has mostly disappeared.

## The people who depend on it entirely

The number in the Money Talks News piece that stopped us: in a 2025 Senior Citizens League survey, 39% of older recipients said Social Security was all of their income. Not most. All.

For those households, Ramsey's warning isn't a lecture, it's a description. And it makes two decisions far more consequential:

- **When to claim.** Claiming at 62 permanently cuts the check by up to 30%. Waiting to 70 raises it by 8% a year past full retirement age. For someone with nothing else, that gap is the difference between getting by and not.
- **What happens in 2032.** The trustees now project the retirement fund runs short in late 2032, after which incoming taxes cover about 78% of benefits unless Congress acts. A household living on the check alone has no cushion for a 22% cut.

> **What to do:** Sign in at ssa.gov/myaccount and look at three numbers: your benefit at 62, at full retirement age, and at 70. If Social Security will be most of your income, the third number is the one to plan toward, and part-time work or savings to bridge the years is how you get there.

## The 15% rule, and where it wobbles

Ramsey's other rule is to save 15% of income for retirement, not counting an employer match. Money Talks News makes the fair point that 15% says nothing about how much income you'll need. Someone who starts at 25 has 40 years of compounding. Someone starting at 55 does not, and a pension, a paid-off house, or an expensive city changes the target completely.

We'd put it this way: 15% is a good habit, not a guarantee. The real question is the gap between your essential expenses and your guaranteed income, and that number is different for every household.

> **What to do:** Add up your essential monthly bills. Subtract your projected Social Security and any pension. Multiply the gap by 12, then by 25. That's roughly the savings needed to cover the gap for a long retirement. If the number is out of reach, the levers are delaying the claim, cutting the fixed bills, and working a few more years, in that order.

## Why the message lands badly, and why it's still right

Ramsey delivers this the way he delivers everything, and a lot of people over 65 hear "you failed." That's not what the data says. It says the system was built as a floor and most people were never told that clearly. A Gallup survey found 69% of non-retirees worry about having enough, including 64% of people who already have a retirement plan. The worry is rational.

The larger point survives the delivery. Treat the monthly benefit as an addition to what you've saved, not as the money that carries the household. If it turns out to be more than that, you're ahead. If it turns out to be all you have, you'll want to have made the claiming decision like it mattered, because it does.
