Dolly Parton was the fourth of twelve children in a one-room cabin in East Tennessee. She is now worth an estimated $450 million, and the music catalog alone has been valued at about $120 million. Feroz Ansari, a certified financial planner who teaches at UC Irvine, used her story in a Kiplinger essay this week to make an argument about what wealth is actually made of. We think the argument is worth more than most retirement advice, and it applies whether your balance has six digits or seven.

Ansari's frame is "total wealth": financial wealth, which is income, investments and property, plus what he calls wisdom wealth, which is knowing yourself, having purpose, resilience, and relationships. Parton built an unusual amount of both. Three of her decisions carry lessons for anyone planning the next 20 years.

## 1. She chose ownership over a paycheck

In the 1960s, before she had a hit, Parton set up a publishing company with her uncle so she would own the copyrights to what she wrote. When Elvis Presley wanted to record "I Will Always Love You," his manager demanded half the publishing rights. She said no, to Elvis, while still building her career.

Decades later Whitney Houston's version became one of the biggest songs ever recorded, and Parton owned the song. Ansari's line: income pays you today, ownership can pay you for decades.

Nobody reading this owns a song catalog. But the principle is the one behind every index fund, every paid-off house, and every small business: things you own and hold for a long time compound. Things you're paid for stop when you stop.

> **What to do:** Ask the question Ansari suggests, "What am I owning?" alongside "What am I earning?" For a retiree that usually means keeping stock ownership in the portfolio (see what Mellody Hobson said this week about [getting too conservative too soon](/articles/mellody-hobson-retirees-too-conservative-too-soon.html)) and being slow to sell assets that can't be bought back.

## 2. She didn't let one income define her

Parton crossed from country to pop, acted, wrote books, licensed products, and put her name and money into Dollywood, which a Tennessee study credits with $1.8 billion a year in economic impact and 23,000 jobs in the county where she grew up poor.

Ansari's point is that diversification isn't only for portfolios. Skills and work are human capital; saving converts it into financial capital; time and compounding make the money work alongside you. For someone approaching retirement, that argues for a part-time or seasonal income in the early years, which does more for a plan's survival than almost any investment choice. It takes pressure off the portfolio exactly when a bad market would hurt most.

## 3. She had failures, and a cushion for them

Her movie "Rhinestone" flopped. A prime-time show was cancelled after a season. Splitting from Porter Wagoner cost an expensive legal settlement. Ansari's observation is that financial wealth gives you the resources to survive setbacks, and wisdom wealth gives you the will to start again.

Translated to a retirement plan: the cash reserve and the guaranteed income are the financial cushion. The habit of treating a bad year as a year and not as a verdict is the other kind.

> **What to do:** Write down, before the next downturn, what you will and won't do when it comes: which bucket you'll spend from, what you won't sell, and when you'll rebalance. Plans made in calm are the wisdom-wealth version of a savings account.

## What the money is for

In 1995 Parton started the Imagination Library, partly because her father never learned to read. It has mailed hundreds of millions of free books to children. Ansari's closing question is the one retirees are best placed to answer: what is all this wealth for? If the balance is the destination, the finish line keeps moving. Someone will always have more.

Most of the advice on this site is about the "how" of retirement money. Parton's story is a reminder that the "why" is the part that makes the how worth doing. Build the portfolio. Then build the life it's supposed to pay for.
