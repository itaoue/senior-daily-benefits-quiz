Mellody Hobson runs Ariel Investments, the Chicago fund manager, and chairs the board of Starbucks. Asked by Kiplinger to name the single biggest mistake that derails retirement savings, she didn't pick overspending or a bad stock. She picked caution.

"People think they are winning by not losing when over time it's not outpacing inflation," she said. Retirees see the finish line and move everything to cash and bonds, and pre-retirees do it even earlier. "People pull back too fast, too soon."

We think she's right, and it's a mistake this site is partly responsible for encouraging. We've spent weeks telling readers to hold two to three years of spending in cash. That advice stands. The mistake Hobson is describing is the other 80% of the portfolio.

## Why "not losing" loses

A 65-year-old woman today can expect to live to about 86 on average, and a quarter of them will reach 92. That's a 20 to 30 year retirement, and over 30 years prices roughly double at ordinary inflation. A portfolio that sits entirely in savings accounts and short-term bonds doesn't lose money on paper. It loses buying power every year, and by the second decade of retirement the loss is large enough to change how you live.

Hobson isn't arguing for aggression. Her prescription is a diversified mix of stocks and fixed income that beats inflation and lasts three decades. The stocks are what do the beating.

> **What to do:** Look at what share of your total savings is in stock funds. If you're under 60 and the number is below 50%, or retired and it's below 30%, Hobson's warning is about you.

## The fix is slow on purpose

The part of Hobson's advice we'd underline is how to correct it. "Don't go crazy buying stocks all at once," she says. Either have an adviser rebalance you, or move money into stock funds in equal monthly amounts over six months to a year. Buying gradually means a bad month right after you start doesn't wreck the plan or your nerve.

She also endorses the bucket approach we've described before: a short-term bucket in cash for the next couple of years, a medium bucket in bonds, and a long-term bucket in growth-oriented stock funds that you don't plan to touch for a decade. The buckets are what let a retiree own stocks without lying awake during a downturn.

> **What to do:** Pick a dollar amount, pick a date each month, and move it from the bond or cash side into a broad index fund until the mix is where you want it. Write the plan down so a market dip in month three doesn't talk you out of month four.

## Two more mistakes she sees

**Taking the 401(k) as a lump sum.** The temptation at retirement is to cash out and buy the boat. Hobson's line: "You don't have to buy the boat; you can go on a boat trip." A lump sum from a traditional 401(k) is taxed as ordinary income in one year, which can push a large withdrawal into the top brackets, and every dollar spent is a dollar that stops compounding. Rolling it into an IRA and drawing what you need keeps the tax bill spread out and the balance working.

**Supporting adult children indefinitely.** Hobson calls it an epidemic. Her advice is to set a clear expectation of what the support is and when it ends, and "take the training wheels off." We covered how to have that conversation in our piece on [an adult child moving back home](/articles/adult-child-moved-back-home-house-rules.html).

## Never too late

Her closing message is the one worth keeping. Retirement is not a finish line, and course corrections don't have to be dramatic. A too-conservative 70-year-old who shifts a fifth of the portfolio into stocks over the next year, keeps two years of spending in cash, and stops writing checks to a 35-year-old has changed the next 20 years without doing anything bold on any single day. "There is no real finish line," Hobson says. That's the good news.
