Barbara Corcoran built a real estate brokerage from a $1,000 loan, sold it for $66 million, and has spent 16 seasons on "Shark Tank" telling entrepreneurs what they're doing wrong. She also, by her own account, refuses to buy a business-class ticket. A GOBankingRates roundup this week collected five things she wants people to stop doing with their money, drawn mostly from her CNBC Make It interviews. The list is aimed at everyone. Four of the five land harder on retirees than on anyone else.

## 1. Stop paying for first class

Corcoran's line is that the money is better invested than spent on a wider seat. She flies coach and has said she will not buy a business-class ticket on a plane.

For a retiree the math is even more lopsided. Upgrades on a couple of trips a year can equal a month of Social Security. The caveat, which the GOBankingRates piece is right to add: if a long flight is genuinely hard on your back or your circulation, that's a health expense, not a luxury. Pay for the legroom, skip the champagne.

> **What to do:** Book economy, then spend a fraction of the difference on the seat selection or extra-legroom row. You get most of the comfort for a tenth of the price.

## 2. Stop buying bottled water

A small habit, and Corcoran calls it out because it's pure waste: a filter pitcher costs less than a month of bottles. At a dollar or two a bottle, a household going through a case a week spends several hundred dollars a year on something that comes out of the tap.

The fair caveat is that tap water quality varies by town and by the age of your plumbing. A filter solves most of that. If your water has a real problem, a home filtration unit is still cheaper over a year than bottles.

## 3. Stop parking money in a checking account

This is the one that matters most after 65, and Corcoran is blunt about it: idle cash loses to inflation every year. What $100 bought in 1970 costs about $850 now. Retirees tend to hold more cash than anyone, for good reasons, and the mistake isn't holding it. It's holding it at 0.1% at a big bank when Treasury bills, money market funds, and online savings accounts pay several percent with the same federal insurance.

> **What to do:** Keep your emergency fund and two to three years of planned withdrawals in cash, but move it to a high-yield account or a Treasury bill ladder. On $100,000 the difference is several thousand dollars a year, and it takes fifteen minutes online. We covered the full picture in [how much cash a retiree should hold](/articles/how-much-cash-should-a-retiree-hold.html).

## 4. Stop ignoring closing costs

Corcoran tells a story about buyers who showed up at closing without the money for closing costs and lost the deal. Closing costs run 2% to 5% of the purchase price, and on a $400,000 house that's $8,000 to $20,000 on top of the down payment.

For retirees this shows up in downsizing. The plan to sell the big house and "free up" money often forgets two sets of closing costs, the agent's commission on the sale, moving, and the repairs a buyer's inspector will demand. Plenty of people finish a downsize with less cash than they started with. Our [downsizing piece](/articles/downsizing-in-retirement-hidden-costs.html) walks through the seven costs that eat the difference.

> **What to do:** Before listing your home or making an offer on a smaller one, ask the agent or lender for a written estimate of every cost on both sides. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's closing disclosure explainer shows what each line means.

## 5. Stop believing money fixes everything

Corcoran has said that money has nothing to do with being happier, and that the point of it is freedom, not a cure for what's bothering you. Coming from someone worth nine figures, that could sound glib. It isn't. Every study of retirement satisfaction finds the same thing: after basic security is covered, health, relationships, and having somewhere to be in the morning predict happiness far better than the balance does.

This is the item that isn't really about money at all, and it's the one retirees are best placed to act on. The savings target is a means. Plan the days, not just the dollars.

## The scorecard

Four of the five are the same advice we'd give anyone living on a fixed income: don't pay for status, don't pay for water, don't let cash rot, and don't get surprised at a closing table. The fifth is a reminder that the goal of all the other four is a life, not a number. Corcoran, who has more money than she can spend and still flies coach, seems to have taken her own advice.
