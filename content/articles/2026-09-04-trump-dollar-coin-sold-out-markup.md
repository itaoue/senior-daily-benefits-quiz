The Treasury's dollar coin bearing Donald Trump's portrait went on sale Wednesday, and by Thursday the Mint's website listed it as unavailable. Rolls of 25 sold for $61, which works out to $2.44 a coin. Bags of 100 went for $154.50, or $1.55 each. A vending machine at the Mint's store in Washington charged $5 a coin. Every one of them has a face value of one dollar.

Money.com covered the launch straight, and the facts are worth laying out plainly, because the resale market is already forming and the people it will be aimed at are the people who read this site.

## What the coin is

Gold-colored, not gold. The core is copper, with a manganese-brass finish, the same recipe as every dollar coin since the Sacagawea in 2000. Trump's portrait is on the front and the presidential seal on the back. It was issued under a 2020 law for the country's 250th anniversary, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has cited a 1926 half dollar showing Calvin Coolidge as precedent for a living president on a coin. Donald Scarinci, a member of the Citizens Coinage Advisory Committee, told reporters that approving it without the committee's review "will be illegal." A court declined to rule on an earlier challenge.

None of that changes what it's made of.

## What it's worth

Three numbers, in order:

- **Metal value:** a few cents.
- **Face value:** one dollar. It's legal tender; you could spend it.
- **What people paid:** $1.55 to $5, and whatever the resale sites ask next week.

The Mint's markup on rolls and bags is normal; it charges a premium on every collectible product. What isn't normal is the sellout, which means the next round of buyers will be paying collectors, not the Mint, and the price will be whatever a stranger online says it is.

> **What to do:** If you want one as a keepsake, wait for the Mint to restock and pay the Mint's price. A dollar coin bought for $2.44 is a souvenir. The same coin bought for $40 from a listing that says "rare" or "sold out at the Mint" is a mistake.

## The collectible question

Will it go up? History with modern circulating-style coins says almost never. The Mint struck presidential dollars for every president from Washington to Reagan between 2007 and 2016; most trade today at roughly face value plus a dollar. Commemoratives hold value only when they're both genuinely scarce and made of precious metal. This coin is neither. A restock will arrive, and the Mint can strike as many as it likes.

The Coolidge half dollar Bessent cited is the cautionary tale: most of the 1926 issue went unsold and was melted down.

## Where the real money changes hands

The coin itself is a small bet. The problem is what gets sold next to it. Search interest in "Trump gold coin" is exactly what precious-metals telemarketers wait for, and the pitch writes itself: patriotism, the $40 trillion debt, a dollar "no longer backed by gold" in the Treasury Secretary's own words, and a "limited" gold-colored coin as the hook. From there it's a short step to graded "collector" coins at three times metal value, or a gold IRA with fees the ad never mentions. State regulators have brought those cases, and the buyers were mostly over 60.

> **What to do:** Three questions end most of these calls. What is the coin's gold content in ounces? What is the price per ounce over today's spot price? Will you put the buyback terms in writing? A dealer who won't answer all three is selling the story, not the metal. Our [guide to coins and gold IRAs](/articles/commemorative-coins-gold-iras-what-to-check.html) has the full checklist.

The Trump dollar is a dollar with a famous face on it. Buy it for a dollar and change, or don't. The only way to lose real money on it is to let someone convince you it's something else.
