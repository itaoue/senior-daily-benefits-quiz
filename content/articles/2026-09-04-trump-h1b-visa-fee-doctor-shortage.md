The proposal from the Department of Homeland Security is a new $103,265 fee for employers who sponsor a worker on an H-1B visa, the visa for foreign professionals in specialty fields. Stacked on the $100,000 fee the administration imposed last year, the cost of bringing in one overseas worker would top $203,000. DHS says the money is "to generate dedicated revenue to support the costs of administering the lawful immigration system" and projects $8.8 billion over a decade.

Most coverage treats this as a story about Amazon, Apple and Google. Money Talks News noticed the other big user of the visa: hospitals and medical research. We think that's the part that matters to people over 65, and it's the part almost nobody is talking about.

## Who actually holds these visas

About one in four physicians practicing in the United States trained abroad, and a large share of them came through the H-1B or a waiver program tied to it. They are disproportionately in the specialties and places that American graduates avoid: internal medicine and geriatrics, nephrology, psychiatry, and the rural and inner-city hospitals that can't compete with suburban salaries. The federal government designates more than 7,000 areas as having a shortage of primary care alone, and foreign-trained doctors are how many of those areas have a doctor at all.

A hospital worker quoted by Money Talks News put it directly: higher fees would "worsen existing national physician shortages, disproportionately harm rural and inner-city communities." Applications under the existing $100,000 fee are already down 87%.

## What that looks like in a small town

A 300-bed regional hospital that recruits four foreign-trained specialists a year would face more than $800,000 in visa fees before paying a salary. Large systems will absorb that. Independent rural hospitals, more than 300 of which are already flagged as at risk of closing after last year's Medicaid cuts, will not. They'll leave the post open, and the patient will drive two hours or wait four months.

If you're on Medicare in a county with one cardiologist, this is the policy that decides whether there's a second one.

> **What to do:** Find out now whether your specialists are employed by an independent hospital or a large system, and whether your county is on the federal shortage list at data.hrsa.gov. If it is, build the relationship with a primary-care office that has telehealth arrangements with a larger system. Waiting until the specialist leaves is the expensive way to learn this.

## The argument on the other side

Supporters of the fee say the H-1B program has been abused to import cheaper workers and that a six-figure price tag will reserve it for jobs that genuinely can't be filled at home. A doctor a rural hospital can't otherwise hire is exactly that job, which is why several proposals in Congress would exempt physicians and nurses from the fee. None has passed. The DHS proposal, as written, has no carve-out for health care.

## What it means for the rest of the health bill

Fewer specialists doesn't just mean longer waits. It means more care delivered in emergency rooms, more transfers to distant hospitals, and higher prices where the remaining doctors have no competition. Those costs flow into Medicare Advantage premiums, Part B costs, and the supplemental policies retirees buy to cover the gaps. A visa fee sold as revenue for the immigration system ends up, in a rural county, as a line in your medical bills.

> **What to do:** The proposal is open for public comment before it takes effect. Comments from patients in shortage areas carry weight, and your representative's office will know the deadline. One paragraph about how far you drive to see a specialist is worth more than a form letter.
