The most common retirement mistake isn't saving too little. It's assuming Social Security will do a job it was never built to do.

For a person with average lifetime earnings, Social Security replaces roughly **40% of pre-retirement income**. Higher earners get a smaller percentage, lower earners a bit more. That's by design: the program was created as a floor, with pensions and savings meant to fill the rest. Pensions have mostly disappeared, and for many households nothing replaced them.

If Social Security is going to be most of your income, the goal is to make that 40% as large as possible and to make the rest of your budget as small as possible. Here are the three moves, in order of impact.

## 1. Get your actual number, not a guess

Log in or create an account at SSA.gov/myaccount and open your Social Security Statement. It shows your estimated monthly benefit at 62, at your full retirement age, and at 70. Most people have never looked, and the gap between those three numbers is usually the biggest financial lever left in their life.

While you're there, check the earnings record for missing years. A single year of earnings that was never posted, common for people who changed names or worked for a small employer that misfiled, can lower your benefit permanently. You can ask SSA to correct it if you have a W-2 or tax return.

## 2. Decide when to claim as a math problem, not a mood

Claiming at 62 instead of your full retirement age (66 or 67, depending on birth year) permanently cuts your check by up to 30%. Waiting from full retirement age to 70 raises it by 8% per year. For a $2,000 full-age benefit, that's the difference between about $1,400 a month at 62 and $2,480 at 70, for the rest of your life, with inflation adjustments on top.

> **What to do:** If you're still working or have savings to bridge a few years, delaying is usually the highest-return "investment" available to you. If you're in poor health or have no other income, claiming earlier can be the right call. Married couples should look at the higher earner delaying, because that benefit becomes the survivor benefit for whoever lives longer.

## 3. Cut the fixed costs that Social Security has to cover

A benefit of $1,900 a month goes much further when the fixed bills underneath it shrink. The biggest ones for most retirees, in order: housing, Medicare and health costs, car, phone and utilities.

- **Housing:** Property tax exemptions for seniors exist in nearly every state and can be worth $500 to $2,000 a year. Most require a one-time application at the county assessor's office.
- **Medicare:** Medicare Savings Programs pay the Part B premium, currently about $185 a month, for people under certain income limits. That's $2,200 a year back in your check.
- **Phone and internet:** Lifeline and carrier 55+ plans can cut a phone bill in half.
- **Everything else:** Ask for the senior discount, everywhere, every time.

Social Security isn't going away, and it isn't going to become enough on its own. Treat it as the base and build the rest deliberately.
